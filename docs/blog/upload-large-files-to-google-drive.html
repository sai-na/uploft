How do I upload large files to Google Drive?

Published 29 October 2026 · Updated 29 October 2026 · By Sai Nath

On a computer, open drive.google.com and click New ▸ File upload, or use Google Drive for desktop to upload in the background. One file can be up to 5 TB, and Google Workspace accounts can upload 750 GB a day. Your upload speed sets the time: 50 GB at 20 Mbps takes about 5.5 hours, so keep the computer awake.

This guide comes from the maker of Uploft, the Chrome extension mentioned below. Google's limits and steps link to Google's own pages and were checked on 29 October 2026; what the extension does comes from its source code.

How to upload a large file from the browser

  1. On your computer, go to drive.google.com.
  2. At the top left, click New ▸ File upload, or Folder upload for a whole folder. You can also drag files into the Drive window.
  3. Choose the file. Drive shows its progress in an upload panel.
  4. Keep the tab open, the computer awake and the connection up until it finishes. The upload runs in that tab, so closing it ends the upload.

For a folder you add to often, Google's Google Drive for desktop (Windows and macOS) is the other built-in way: it puts a "Google Drive" folder on your computer, and files you drag into it upload to Drive without a browser tab.

What is the largest file Google Drive accepts?

5 TB per file, according to Google's list of files you can store in Drive. Files converted to Google's own formats have lower limits: a text document converted to Google Docs can be up to 50 MB, and spreadsheets and presentations converted to Sheets or Slides up to 100 MB. Videos, archives, disk images and other files kept as they are only have the 5 TB limit.

The file also has to fit in your storage. A personal Google Account includes up to 15 GB, shared by Drive, Gmail and Photos, and once it is full, Google says you won't be able to upload files to Drive.

Is there a daily upload limit?

Yes, for Google Workspace accounts: 750 GB within 24 hours, across My Drive and shared drives, copies included. Users who reach it can't upload or copy more until the limit refreshes, within 24 hours. A single file bigger than 750 GB can still finish, because Google says the first file that breaks the limit completes uploading. Google's help page for personal accounts lists only the 5 TB file limit.

The daily limit only matters on fast connections. Uploading 750 GB in 24 hours means 750 × 8,000 = 6,000,000 megabits in 86,400 seconds, which needs about 69 Mbps of upload speed the whole day. On a slower connection, the day runs out before the limit does.

How long will a large upload take?

Divide the size in megabits by your upload speed in megabits per second. One gigabyte is 1,000 megabytes and one megabyte is 8 megabits, so 1 GB is 8,000 megabits.

Worked example: a 50 GB file on a 20 Mbps upload. 50 × 8,000 = 400,000 megabits, and 400,000 ÷ 20 = 20,000 seconds, which is 5 hours 33 minutes.

These are best cases. Use your upload speed, not the download speed a provider advertises: home connections often upload much slower than they download. Other traffic on the same connection adds time.

Can you make one big upload faster?

Not by much. The time is set by your connection's upload speed, and the most that helps is giving the upload all of it: a wired connection or a spot near the router, and pausing other uploads and backups on the same network.

Splitting one file across several connections isn't possible with Drive's resumable uploads. Google's upload guide has each piece start where Drive's reply says the last one ended, so a file's pieces go up one after another. Batches of many files are different: each file has its own start and finish, so uploading several at the same time can save time on a folder of many small and medium files.

How do I upload big files without watching them?

Two tools keep going without you:

Google Drive for desktop

Google's own app syncs a folder on your computer with Drive in the background, and you can pause and resume the sync. It suits files you want kept in sync.

Uploft

A free Chrome extension whose uploader page is built for one-off big uploads from the browser. Click its toolbar icon, then Upload files to Drive, sign in with Google and drop files or folders in.

  • It sends 1 to 6 files at a time, 4 by default, set under At a time.
  • Big files go up in pieces that start at 8 MB. A piece that finishes in under 4 seconds makes the next one twice as big, up to 64 MB; one that takes over 20 seconds halves it. Big pieces mean fewer round trips on a fast line; small ones lose less when a slow line drops.
  • After a Wi-Fi drop, a closed tab, a crash or a restart, it continues from the last byte Drive confirmed instead of starting again.
  • While uploads run, it keeps the computer from sleeping and Chrome from unloading the tab.
  • Folders keep their structure, and uploading the same folder again skips the files it already put there.

For one huge file it uploads at about the same speed as Drive's own site. The difference is what happens when something interrupts it: it carries on from where it was, not from zero. How resuming works.

Limits to know

  • The uploader runs in a Chrome tab. Closing the tab stops its uploads; when you open it again, they continue from where they stopped after you press Resume or choose the files again.
  • Drive keeps an unfinished upload for one week. After that, the file starts again from zero.
  • Google's 750 GB daily limit and your storage still apply.
  • It can only see files it uploaded and folders you choose for it, because it uses Google's narrowest Drive permission (drive.file). So "skip what's already there" only knows its own uploads.
  • It runs in Chrome 116 or later on a computer, and it isn't on the Chrome Web Store yet.

Uploft is free, and its source code is public. It isn't on the Chrome Web Store yet; the home page shows what it does and what it asks for.
