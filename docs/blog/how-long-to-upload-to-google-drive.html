How long does it take to upload to Google Drive?

Published 29 October 2026 · Updated 29 October 2026 · By Sai Nath

Multiply the file size in gigabytes by 8,000 to get megabits, then divide by your upload speed in Mbps to get seconds. 100 GB at 20 Mbps is 800,000 ÷ 20 = 40,000 seconds, about 11 hours. The speed that matters is the upload figure, and Drive's daily ceiling can also apply.

This guide comes from the maker of Uploft, the Chrome extension mentioned below. Google's limits link to Google's own pages and were checked on 29 October 2026; what the extension does comes from its source code. The times are arithmetic, not measured speeds.

How do you work out the upload time?

Take three steps. Speed tests report megabits per second (Mbps); file sizes are in bytes, and a byte is 8 bits. These sums use decimal units, where 1 GB is 1,000 MB.

  1. Read your upload speed. Run a speed test and use the upload figure, not the download one. Say it shows 20 Mbps.
  2. Convert the file to megabits. 100 GB is 100 × 1,000 MB × 8 = 800,000 megabits.
  3. Divide by the speed. 800,000 ÷ 20 Mbps = 40,000 seconds. Divided by 3,600, that is 11.1 hours, about 11 hours 7 minutes.

In one line: seconds = GB × 8,000 ÷ Mbps. Your plan's headline speed is often the download figure, so use the upload line. The result is the best case, because it assumes the connection runs at that speed the whole time. Real uploads usually take a little longer, and much longer when something else is using the connection. For the causes, see why a Google Drive upload can be slow.

How long do 1, 10, 100 and 750 GB take?

At 20 Mbps, 1 GB takes about 7 minutes, 10 GB about 1 hour 7 minutes, 100 GB about 11 hours and 750 GB about 3 days 11 hours. The table applies the same sum, seconds = GB × 8,000 ÷ Mbps, to four speeds.

Does Drive limit how much you can upload in a day?

Yes for Google Workspace users: 750 GB a day. Google's Drive API limits page says Workspace users can upload 750 GB per day between My Drive and all shared drives, that the limit also applies to copies, and that a user who reaches it cannot upload or copy more files until 24 hours have passed (see Google's Drive API limits page). Google's help page for personal accounts lists a 5 TB file limit and gives no daily figure, so check your own account type.

The arithmetic shows when the ceiling matters. To send 750 GB inside 24 hours you need 750 × 8,000 ÷ 86,400 seconds = about 69.4 Mbps, held all day. At 20 Mbps you could send about 216 GB in a day (20 × 86,400 ÷ 8,000), so the connection is the limit long before Drive is. The 750 GB row in the table is a reason to plan a multi-day job, not to expect it to finish in one go. More in the daily upload limit guide and the limits table.

Why does the time left keep changing?

Because any estimate is the remaining size divided by a recent speed, and the speed moves. Uploft works it out this way: the speed is the data sent over the last 5 seconds, and the time left is the bytes still to send divided by that speed. Take 6 GB left. At 20 MB/s that reads about 5 minutes (6,000 ÷ 20 = 300 seconds). If the speed dips to 10 MB/s the same 6 GB reads about 10 minutes, and when it recovers the figure shrinks again.

Common reasons the speed moves:

  • Wi-Fi signal that varies from minute to minute.
  • Other devices or apps sharing the upload bandwidth.
  • A VPN or a busy network at certain times of day.
  • Files finishing and the next ones starting, which briefly changes how many streams are running.

Treat an early estimate as rough. It settles as the upload runs and the speed levels out.

Is there an upload time calculator?

You already have one: the sum in the first section. Put the size in GB and the upload speed in Mbps into GB × 8,000 ÷ Mbps, then divide by 3,600 for hours. Example: 250 GB at 50 Mbps is 250 × 8,000 ÷ 50 = 40,000 seconds, about 11 hours 7 minutes. For a file in MB, use MB × 8 ÷ Mbps instead.

How does Uploft help on a long upload?

Uploft is a free Chrome extension (Chrome 116 or later) with its own uploader page. It cannot make your connection faster, so the table above still holds. It helps with the waiting.

  • A live estimate. The uploader shows the speed and the time left, so you can compare them with your own sum.
  • Notifications. It sends a notification when uploads finish and one for each file that fails, so you can leave the tab.
  • Keep-awake. The setting Keep the computer awake while uploading is on by default, so an idle computer does not go to sleep in the middle of an 11-hour upload.
  • Resuming. Uploads continue after network drops and can be resumed after a closed tab or a restart, so a long job does not start from zero.

What can’t it do?

  • Raise your upload speed. One file goes up at about the speed of Drive's own site, because the pieces of a single file are sent in order and your connection is the limit.
  • Get past Drive's daily limit. When Drive reports it, the uploader stops and says so.
  • Promise to keep a laptop awake with the lid closed. That depends on the computer's own settings, and a sleeping computer cannot upload until it wakes.
  • It runs in Chrome on a computer, not on phones, and it isn't on the Chrome Web Store yet.

Uploft is free, and its source code is public. It isn't on the Chrome Web Store yet; the home page shows what it does and what it asks for.
